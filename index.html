The divine knowledge

Why the Kumbh happens

The story behind the world's largest pilgrimage — told as tradition holds it, with the astronomy that still sets its dates.

I

The churning of the cosmic ocean

Long ago, the devas (gods) had grown weak and the asuras (demons) strong. To restore the balance, the two rivals agreed to an impossible task: churn the ocean of milk for Amrit — the nectar of immortality. They uprooted Mount Mandara as the churning rod and coiled the serpent-king Vasuki around it as the rope. Vishnu himself, in his Kurma (tortoise) avatar, dove beneath the mountain to hold it steady on his back.

For a thousand years the churning went on. The ocean yielded poison — which Shiva drank to protect the worlds — and then treasures: the moon, the celestial horse, the goddess of fortune, and finally the divine physician Dhanvantari, rising from the waters holding a kumbh, a golden pot of Amrit.

The pot of nectar — the kumbh — gives the festival its name.

II

Twelve days of flight, four drops of nectar

The moment the pot appeared, the truce shattered. The asuras seized it, and a chase across the heavens began. Vishnu took the form of Mohini, an enchantress, and — as tradition tells — reclaimed the pot and fled with it across the sky, distributing the nectar only to the devas.

During that flight, drops of Amrit are believed to have fallen to Earth at four places: Prayagraj, Haridwar, Nashik (Trimbakeshwar) and Ujjain. The chase is said to have lasted twelve divine days — twelve years in human reckoning — which is why the Kumbh returns on a twelve-year cycle, and why the rivers at these four places are held to carry the potency of the nectar itself. To bathe in them, at the right alignment of the planets, is to touch the amrit.

III

The sky clock: how the dates are set

Kumbh timing is not a committee's calendar — it is astronomy. The dates follow the positions of Brihaspati (Jupiter), the Sun and the Moon, mirroring the alignment under which the drops fell. Each city has its own rule:

The 6, 12 and 144-year cycles

6

Ardh Kumbh

The "half" Kumbh, held every six years at Haridwar and Prayagraj — the half-way point of Jupiter's zodiac journey.

12

Purna Kumbh

Jupiter takes about twelve years to circle the zodiac. Each sacred city holds its full Kumbh when its alignment returns — the source of the twelve-year cycle.

144

Maha Kumbh

Once in 144 years at Prayagraj, the alignments recur in an exceptionally rare combination. The 2025 Maha Kumbh was celebrated in this light.

IV

A living tradition, older than memory

The mela's antiquity is documented: the 7th-century Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsang) described a great month-long gathering at Prayaga under Emperor Harsha, with kings bathing and giving alms. In the 8th century CE, Adi Shankaracharya is credited with organising the ascetic orders — the akharas — and formalising the mela as a periodic assembly of sadhus and scholars.

Today the gathering still runs on that ancient architecture: the Dhwajarohan (flag-hoisting) opens the mela; the thirteen akharas march in the Shahi Snan (royal baths), the Nagas first; lakhs of Kalpvasis keep their month of riverside austerity; and free langar kitchens feed millions. In 2017, UNESCO inscribed the Kumbh Mela on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Akharas & Shahi Snan

The 13 monastic orders lead the royal bath processions — a fixed order of precedence, led by the Naga sadhus, that has been negotiated over centuries.

Kalpvas

Devotees camp on the riverbank for the whole month of Magh — sleeping, eating and meditating by the river, in the belief that one kalpa of merit is earned.

Dhwajarohan

The hoisting of the flag formally begins the mela; its lowering months later closes it.

The temporary city

Each Kumbh builds one of the world's largest temporary cities — hospitals, police stations, pontoon bridges and tens of thousands of shelters rising on empty sandbanks.

Words of the sacred texts

What the Vedas, Puranas and epics say

The churning of the ocean, the pot of amrit, and the glory of the four tirthas — traced text by text through the Vedic and Puranic literature.

The voice of the tradition: the churning of the ocean and the pot of amrit are narrated in the Puranas and the epics; the falling of the four drops is the living heart of the Kumbh — carried through the ages by saints, mahatmas and the akharas, from Adi Shankaracharya to the sages of today. Some truths the shastras declare in words; others are realised in the experience of the bath. We present both — with reverence.

Reach the river

Plan your pilgrimage

City-by-city directions and budget-friendly ways to reach the ghats. All booking links open on external services — the mela's own accommodation (tents, dharamshalas, state tourism) is booked through official state portals.

The budget pilgrim's playbook

Golden rule: travel mid-mela, not on a Shahi Snan peak. Room rates near the ghats can double or triple on the biggest bathing days, and trains sell out weeks ahead.

Book the train early

Indian Railways opens bookings ~60 days out (ARP) and runs thousands of special mela trains — over 16,000 ran during Maha Kumbh 2025. Use the Vikalp (alternative train) option while waiting.

Stay 10–20 km out

Hotels closest to the ghats command a premium. Staying a little away and using the free mela shuttle buses — or walking the pontoon routes at dawn — saves hugely.

Dharamshalas & tents

Dharamshalas, ashrams and state-tourism tent cities are a fraction of hotel prices. Book only through official state portals and verified trusts.

Fly via a nearby city

Mela-city flights are regulated around peak days but still scarce. Check nearby airports — Mumbai for Nashik, Indore for Ujjain, Dehradun for Haridwar — and finish by train or bus.

Eat langar, carry UPI

Free langar meals run at akhara and seva camps. Carry cash and UPI both — networks at remote stall clusters can drop.

Arrive overnight

Overnight trains arrive at dawn — bathe, and leave the same day to skip the costliest night of all.

Stay safe at the ghats

  • On peak snan days, keep to the edges of crowds, follow the pontoon-bridge routes and note the designated holding areas.
  • Emergency helpline in India: 112. Lost-person camps and women-and-child help kiosks operate across the mela grounds.
  • Verify prices before booking any stay — mela season attracts fraud along with legitimate demand. Prefer official portals.
  • Dawn is the auspicious hour for the snan — and the safest, before the day's crowds build.
